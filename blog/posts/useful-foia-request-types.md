{{MODULE:jumplist}}

Final reports tell the public what an office concluded. Recurring internal reports, group mailing lists, and internal web pages show how it got there: who asked for what, how often, who is on the distribution, and which systems the staff uses. These records are written on a schedule or kept in predictable places, and they can be requested by date range, custodian, or name, which makes them productive targets under the Freedom of Information Act.

This guide covers four record types, each drawn from a release obtained under FOIA: weekly activity reports from the Defense Department's Office of Inspector General, White House email held by the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative, a White House distribution-list invitation held by the Small Business Administration, and intranet pages from the Justice Department's Justice Management Division. The Defense Department release consists of four weekly activity reports that the inspector general's Office of General Counsel sent to the inspector general in April 2024, and its 18 released pages count subpoenas week by week, by requesting agency, and by type of investigation. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=1))

NOTE: The DoD records, case DODOIG-2024-000442, were obtained under FOIA and are [hosted with this guide](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf). OctoProbe blacked out the requester's name, email address, and street address on the response letter; every other redaction is the agency's. The other three records are hosted as released, compressed for the web; none contained requester information. Page citations give the page of the hosted PDF.

## Type 1: Inspector General Weekly Activity Reports and Info Memos

A weekly activity report is a short memo in which an office tells its leadership what it did that week and what is coming. In the Defense Department inspector general's office, the general counsel's weekly reports went to the inspector general as "INFO MEMO" documents titled "Weekly Activity Report" for a stated date range. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=3)) Each released report follows the same outline: "Priorities," "Significant Activities and Events," "90 Day Outlook," "Other Issues and Matters of Interest," "Weekly Subpoena Support," and "Personnel Actions." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=3))

{{MODULE:fig-memo}}

The April 2024 reports were signed by Paul Hadjiyane, general counsel, for the weeks of April 1, April 8, and April 15, and by David A. Core, principal deputy general counsel, for the week of April 22. Each lists Core as the preparer. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=17)) Requests for them go to the Defense Department inspector general's FOIA office, which processes requests for the inspector general's own records and says it does not handle requests for the rest of the department. ([FOIA](https://www.dodig.mil/FOIA/))

Because each report is dated and repeats the same sections, a run of them can be lined up week against week. The most useful recurring section in this release is the subpoena table. Every report includes a "Weekly Subpoena Support" table listing a count for each Defense Department investigative agency, and the four April 2024 tables total 35, 31, 36, and 55 subpoenas. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=19)) Each table's footnote gives the number "of the total issued subpoenas" that were "for Basic Subscriber Information": 14, 20, 20, and 25. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=11))

Added together, the tables count 157 subpoenas over the four weeks, 79 of them, or 50%, for basic subscriber information. In the week of April 8, 20 of 31 subpoenas, or 65%, were for subscriber information. The records do not say whether the weekly tables capture every subpoena the office issued in a given week.

A note says the "Calculation excludes weekends and holidays." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=7))

{{MODULE:fig-times}}

The report also says the office "provided training to 177 DCIO Investigators in March 2024," referring to Defense criminal investigative organizations. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=7)) The April 12 report lists training at the Federal Law Enforcement Training Centers on April 23 "on requesting and obtaining subpoenas and other criminal law matters." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=10))

The subpoenas rest on the Inspector General Act, now codified at 5 U.S.C. § 406, formerly Section 6 of the 1978 act. It lets an inspector general "require by subpoena the production of all information, documents, reports, answers, records, accounts, papers, and other data in any medium," enforceable in federal district court, but directs that "procedures other than subpoenas" be used to get records from federal agencies. ([statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/406)) At the Defense Department, "The Subpoena Program Director administers all subpoena requests by the Defense Criminal Investigative Organizations (DCIOs)," according to the inspector general's website. ([OIG](https://www.dodig.mil/Programs/Subpoena-Program/))

The released reports do not define "Basic Subscriber Information." Federal law lists the subscriber records that a provider of electronic communication or remote computing service must disclose when the government uses "an administrative subpoena authorized by a Federal or State statute": name; address; connection records or "records of session times and durations"; length and types of service; subscriber numbers or identities, "including any temporarily assigned network address"; and "means and source of payment." ([law](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2703)) The same section says the government "is not required to provide notice to a subscriber or customer." ([law](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2703)) Where these requests sit among other legal process is covered in a separate [guide](/blog/subpoena-warrant-online-accounts-three-branches/).

The reports name few investigative subjects. Under "Significant Activities and Events," the April 19 report lists "Support to ISO in [withheld] Investigation," saying office lawyers "spoke with the investigative team" and are "consulting with ISO on next steps." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=14)) ISO is the inspector general's Investigations of Senior Officials directorate. ([OIG](https://www.dodig.mil/Components/Administrative-Investigations/Investigations-of-Senior-Officials/)) The April 26 report says the lawyers "reviewed the comments from IGFO on the draft ROI," a report of investigation whose subject is withheld under (b)(6), "and met with ISO to discuss." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=18))

Other released items include a case in federal district court in Washington, its name withheld, in which the office is "working with DoD OGC and the DC USAO to determine next steps," and *Day v. DoD*, a FOIA lawsuit in which "The court ordered the OIG to review redactions taken under exception 7(D)" on 10 pages of records. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=14)) The April 19 and April 26 reports also list the "Audit of DoD's Implementation of the Defense Cooperation Agreement (DCA) with the State of Kuwait (Project No. D2024-D000RJ-0080.00)," with the substance withheld. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=13)) Repeated entries cover legal support to the Office of Legislative Affairs and Communications, the inspector general's congressional and media office, and to Administrative Investigations. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=8)) ([OLAC](https://www.dodig.mil/About/Offices/Legislative-Affairs-and-Communications/))

Staffing appears in every report. The general counsel's office listed 41 civilian positions under "AES (UpperLimit)" and an "End Strength" of 37, with four accepted offers by April 19. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=16)) Each of these items, from a named audit to a withheld investigation title, is a lead that a narrower follow-up request can cite by name and date.

The request behind this release sought "[a]ll Weekly Activity Reports (or Info Memos) or finalized weekly reporting to the DOD Inspector General from the Office of General Counsel" for April 2024. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=1)) The office received it April 29, 2024, and answered July 10, 2024. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=1)) A follow-up can name the same office, recipient, record type, and a short window, for example "all finalized weekly activity reports, info memos, or other recurring weekly reporting submitted to the Inspector General by the Office of General Counsel between [start date] and [end date], including attachments, tables, and charts." Asking for "finalized" versions steers the search away from drafts, which agencies often withhold as deliberative, and a window of a month or a quarter makes gaps between requests easy to spot. Filing basics are covered in a separate [how-to](/blog/foia-how-to-obama-situation-room-photos-presidential-records/).

Fees and speed are set by statute. For a representative of the news media, fees are limited to "reasonable standard charges for document duplication." ([statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/552)) A requester can seek expedited processing by showing a "compelling need," which for a person primarily engaged in disseminating information includes an "urgency to inform the public concerning actual or alleged Federal Government activity." ([statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/552)) The inspector general's response said an appeal "must be postmarked within 90 days of the date of this letter" and pointed requesters to its FOIA Public Liaison and to the Office of Government Information Services at the National Archives. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=2)) ([OGIS](https://www.archives.gov/ogis)) The department's FOIA appeal rules are in 32 C.F.R. part 286. ([eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-II/subchapter-D/part-286))

The response withheld information under (b)(5), "certain inter-and intra-agency communications protected by the attorney-client privilege and the deliberative process privilege," and (b)(6), information whose release "would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=1)) In practice, (b)(5) covered the substance of most legal advice and every subpoena category, while (b)(6) covered staff names, phone numbers, and the names in investigation titles. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=4)) The letter said the review applied the "foreseeable harm standard," which the FOIA statute writes as withholding only where "the agency reasonably foresees that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=1)) ([statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/552))

Reports that touch open cases can also draw the law enforcement exemptions in 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7), including (A), records that "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings"; (C), an "unwarranted invasion of personal privacy"; and (E), records that "would disclose techniques and procedures for law enforcement investigations or prosecutions." ([statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/552)) None of the (b)(7) exemptions was cited in this release. The letter carried the standard exclusion notice: "Congress excluded three discrete categories of law enforcement and national security records from the requirements of the FOIA. See 5 U.S.C. 552(c)." The office called it "a standard notification that is given to all our requesters." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=1)) The Justice Department's FOIA guide covers the exclusions in a separate chapter. ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/oip/foia-guide/exclusions))

The full response ran 20 pages: a two-page letter and 18 pages of records released in part, the four weekly activity reports dated April 5, 12, 19, and 26, 2024. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=1)) The letter was signed by Mark Dorgan, director of the inspector general's FOIA, Privacy and Civil Liberties Office. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/dodoig-2024-000442.pdf#page=2))

The subpoena counts in the weekly reports do not appear in the inspector general's public semiannual report for the same period. A text search of the October 2023 to March 2024 report to Congress finds no use of the word "subpoena." ([SAR](https://www.oversight.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/2024-06/SARSpring-2024signed.pdf))

## Type 2: White House Emails, Requested From the Agency Side

Email that the White House Office, the Office of the Vice President, and other parts of the Executive Office of the President exchange with department and agency officials can be requested from the agency's copy rather than from the White House. The White House Office's own files generally sit outside FOIA. The Justice Department's FOIA guide says the Office of the President, including the "President’s immediate personal staff or units in the Executive Office whose sole function is to advise and assist the President," is not an agency under FOIA. ([guide](https://www.justice.gov/oip/page/file/1199421/dl?inline=)) An earlier edition of the guide traces that line through *Kissinger v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press*, *Meyer v. Bush*, and *Armstrong v. Executive Office of the President*. ([guide](https://www.justice.gov/archive/oip/foia_guide09/procedural-requirements.pdf))

Those offices' records fall under the Presidential Records Act, which covers materials "created or received by the President, the President’s immediate staff, or a unit or individual of the Executive Office of the President whose function is to advise or assist the President." The same definition excludes "official records of an agency." ([statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/44/2201)) A copy of a White House email that reaches a department is therefore generally a record of that department, outside the Presidential Records Act, and requesters can ask the department for it.

Some units inside the Executive Office are agencies in their own right. The Justice Department's guide names the Office of Management and Budget and the Office of Science and Technology Policy among them. ([guide](https://www.justice.gov/oip/page/file/1199421/dl?inline=)) The Office of the U.S. Trade Representative, whose addresses end in ustr.eop.gov, runs its own FOIA office under a published rule. ([rule](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-15/subtitle-C/chapter-XX/part-2004/subpart-B/section-2004.3)) There are limits: the Justice Department's guide cites a federal appeals court ruling that White House visitor logs held by the Secret Service were not "agency records." ([guide](https://www.justice.gov/oip/page/file/1199421/dl?inline=))

At a receiving agency, such email usually passes through the offices of the secretary or agency head, the chief of staff, and the executive secretariat, which route correspondence. The trade representative's FOIA rule asks requesters seeking correspondence to name "the subject matter and the relevant parties with contact information such as their email addresses." ([rule](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-15/subtitle-C/chapter-XX/part-2004/subpart-B/section-2004.3))

The release from the trade representative's office runs 59 pages of email and attachments from Trade Representative Katherine Tai's account, dated from December 2023 through January 2024, released in part. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/ustr-tai-email-release.pdf#page=1)) It shows what the White House tells the Cabinet, in what words, and when. One email is a January 9, 2024, memorandum to Cabinet members from White House Chief of Staff Jeff Zients on "Cabinet Notifications," sent with the note "Please see attached, which requires your immediate attention and response." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/ustr-tai-email-release.pdf#page=1)) The memo directs agencies to submit their delegation-of-authority protocols to the White House Office of Cabinet Affairs by January 12, 2024. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/ustr-tai-email-release.pdf#page=2))

Another email, sent January 24, 2024, at 5:57 p.m., carries "Talking Points on the Conflict in the Middle East" from Evan M. Ryan of the White House Office to "Cabinet members." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/ustr-tai-email-release.pdf#page=18)) Ryan is identified as Cabinet secretary in the January 9 memo. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/ustr-tai-email-release.pdf#page=2)) The distribution names four officials: Jared Bernstein of the Council of Economic Advisers, Shalanda D. ([HUD](https://archives.hud.gov/news/2024/pr24-048.cfm)) The talking points themselves refer to "Secretary Blinken." ([Cabinet](https://web.archive.org/web/20240124022418/https://www.whitehouse.gov/administration/cabinet/)) The agency left these addressees unredacted on the talking-points email, while it withheld most email addresses elsewhere in the same release under Exemption 6. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/ustr-tai-email-release.pdf#page=1))

An address list like this one identifies custodians for later requests, since each address is a mailbox an agency can be asked to search. A request to a department can name the custodians, the date range, and the White House domains, and ask for the agency's own copies with full header information, for example all emails sent to or received by "[the Secretary], [the Chief of Staff], and [the Executive Secretariat] between [start date] and [end date] in which the To, From, Cc, or Bcc field contains an address ending in ‘@who.eop.gov,’ ‘@ovp.eop.gov,’ or ‘@eop.gov.’" Asking for the agency's own copies keeps the request on records the department holds, which are outside the Presidential Records Act.

This release marks its withholdings "EXEMPTION 5," "EXEMPTION 6," and "EXEMPTIONS 5, 6," for deliberative material and for names and addresses. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/ustr-tai-email-release.pdf#page=4)) Requests that touch White House material can also take time for consultation. An April 15, 2009, memo from White House Counsel Gregory Craig told agencies they "should consult with the White House Counsel's Office on all document requests that may involve documents with White House equities," including FOIA requests. ([memo](https://www.justice.gov/oip/guidance/craig_memoranda_4-15-2009.pdf/dl?inline=)) The Justice Department's Office of Information Policy describes consultation as the usual practice when a record "contain[s] within [it] information of interest to another agency." ([OIP](https://www.justice.gov/oip/blog/referrals-consultations-and-coordination-procedures-processing-records-when-another-agency)) The file as released contains no response letter.

## Type 3: Distribution Lists That Reach Core Staff

Email distribution lists are the group addresses an office uses to reach a whole staff or a class of employees at once. A request that names a list address as a search term can return everything sent to that group in a custodian's mailbox, and every mailbox that receives list traffic holds a copy, so agency staff on a White House list, or senior officials on an agency's own list, have records their agency can search.

Some lists are built around employee categories. The Senior Executive Service "includes most managerial, supervisory, and policy positions classified above General Schedule (GS) grade 15 or equivalent positions in the Executive Branch," according to the Office of Personnel Management. ([OPM](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/senior-executive-service/overview-history/)) Political appointees are catalogued in the Plum Book, which "lists over 7,000 Federal civil service leadership and support positions" that "may be subject to noncompetitive appointment." ([Plum](https://www.govinfo.gov/collection/plum-book))

The Small Business Administration released five pages: the invitation, its forwarded copy, and the calendar details. A banner on the second page reads "CAUTION - The sender of this message is external to the SBA network." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/sba-white-house-distribution-list-release.pdf#page=2)) The invitation, from Rachel I. Chiu of the White House Office and dated February 16, 2024, is for a farewell event on February 23, 2024, at 5 p.m. in "Cab Camp (EEOB 160)." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/sba-white-house-distribution-list-release.pdf#page=4))

{{MODULE:fig-dl-invite}}

Its attendee list opens with four distribution lists: "DL OPSO Department," "IGADepartment," "DL WHO OPEAllStaff," and "DL WHO Schedvance." One address is printed in full: DLOPSODepartment@WHO.eop.gov. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/sba-white-house-distribution-list-release.pdf#page=4)) The record does not spell out the lists' abbreviations or say who belongs to them.

{{MODULE:fig-dl-header}}

The same lists appear in the message header on the release's first page, where the agency blacked out their addresses. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/sba-white-house-distribution-list-release.pdf#page=1)) Addresses at the Office of the Vice President, the National Security Council, and the Office of Management and Budget were left visible in the header, while most White House Office addresses were blacked out. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/sba-white-house-distribution-list-release.pdf#page=1)) Those redactions carry no exemption labels. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/sba-white-house-distribution-list-release.pdf#page=1)) Exemption 6, the usual basis for withholding individual addresses, applies to files whose release "would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy." ([statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/552))

A list address printed in a release becomes a search term for the next request. Such a request can name the custodians and the period and ask for "all emails and calendar invitations received by [custodians] between [start date] and [end date] that were addressed to the distribution list [list name or address]," along with records sufficient to show the name, address, and description of each list an office uses to reach its political appointees or Senior Executive Service members. The file as released contains no response letter.

## Type 4: Intranet Pages and Screenshots

Intranet captures show an agency's internal website: home pages, office landing pages, menus, site maps, and the portals for internal tools. FOIA's definition of a record includes information "maintained by an agency in any format, including an electronic format." ([statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/552)) The pages are kept by the office that runs the intranet, usually a chief information officer's staff, and by each office that publishes on it.

What they offer is names: of internal systems, databases, document libraries, offices, and roles. Each name is a hook for a narrower follow-up request that cites the exact system or office, and together they map how an agency is organized from the inside. The same approach underlies a separate [guide](/blog/secret-service-foia-sharepoint-browser-history-osint-guide/) to SharePoint and browser-history requests.

The release comes from the Justice Department's Justice Management Division, which "provides advice and counsel to senior management officials relating to all administrative functions of the Department." ([JMD](https://www.justice.gov/jmd)) It runs 39 pages: screenshots of the division's DOJNet pages, staff profiles from its public website, the cover of the "Justice Management Division Correspondence Style Guide," and the "JMD Sitemap." ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/doj-jmd-intranet-release.pdf#page=38)) The file as released contains no response letter. The division's page on DOJNet, the department's intranet, lists its staffs in a left-hand menu and links to an organizational chart, a document library, and internal tools. ([record](/blog/useful-foia-request-types/assets/doj-jmd-intranet-release.pdf#page=1))
