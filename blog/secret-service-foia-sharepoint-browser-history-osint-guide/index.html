How to FOIA an Official's Browser History, SharePoint Files and Teams Messages

A seven-step guide to finding an agency's SharePoint, naming a custodian and requesting web logs, documents and Teams messages under the Freedom of Information Act, with Secret Service records as the worked example.

Oct 20, 2026
Public Journalism GuideFOIAOSINTSecret ServiceMicrosoft 365

By Colin Aamot

Share

How do you request a federal official’s browser history, SharePoint files or Teams messages under FOIA?

Find the agency’s real Microsoft 365 systems with public DNS and Microsoft’s sign-in metadata, name a custodian by name and title, and ask for each record type by the system that holds it. Results vary. The Secret Service processed requests for five senior officials’ browser histories and released records in part, but a federal appeals court has held that browsing histories an agency neither keeps nor uses are not agency records.

What you can request

  • Browser history and web-proxy, gateway, DNS or endpoint logs
  • SharePoint and OneDrive documents a custodian created or edited
  • Teams chats (Exchange Online mailbox), channel posts (team mailbox), channel files (SharePoint) and chat files (OneDrive)
  • Agency card statements
  • A sitemap of the agency’s SharePoint

You can ask a federal agency for an official’s web activity, the files the official made and the chats the official sent. Requests for those records can come back empty when they don’t name the system, the person or the place the data lives. This guide fixes that in seven steps, with copy-paste language for each record.

The method is a loop. Public internet records tell you where an agency keeps its files. A records request names those systems and a custodian. Each release then hands you new names, offices and systems for the next request.

The worked example throughout is two rounds of requests to the U.S. Secret Service, in 2023 and 2025. Here is what one page of the first round looks like.

Example

The page that started this guide

The page is a printout of a computer log. Each row has a timestamp, a username that begins “ron.rowe” and a box stamped “(b)(6); (b)(7)(C)” where something else used to be. Fifteen rows at 2:16 p.m. on Oct. 10, 2023, list Microsoft 365 servers, among them unitedstatessecretservice.sharepoint.com. The rest, logged between 10:33 and 10:38 p.m. on Oct. 4, list the Daily Mail, the New York Post and a run of advertising exchanges.

The user was Ronald L. Rowe Jr., who had been the agency’s deputy director since April 2023, according to the Homeland Security Department. He went on to run the agency as acting director after Kimberly Cheatle resigned in July 2024. A federal law-enforcement agency treated a senior official’s web activity as a record it could search, review and release.

Key Takeaways

  • You can confirm an agency’s SharePoint address with public DNS and Microsoft’s own sign-in metadata. No login, no scanning.
  • You don’t need an email address. A name and title are enough for the agency to search a custodian’s records.
  • Microsoft documents where Teams chats, channel posts and shared files are kept. A request that names those places is harder to answer with “no records.”
  • The D.C. Circuit held in 2021 that two other agencies’ browsing histories were not agency records. The Secret Service processed requests for five top officials’ browser histories and closed all five “Granted/Denied in Part,” according to its FY 2024 FOIA log.
  • An April 2025 request returned a sitemap of roughly 660 internal Secret Service sites and 105 pages of blacked-out card statements. The PDF contains no records for the two document requests.

How does the OSINT–FOIA loop work?

Records requests fail for boring reasons. Yours names the wrong office. It asks for “all communications” and gets a steep fee estimate. It asks for “SharePoint files” and gets “no records,” because nobody searched SharePoint.

Open-source intelligence, the research discipline built on public information, fixes much of that. It can tell you the name of an agency’s Microsoft 365 tenant, which cloud it runs in, which offices have their own sites and who holds which job. A request that carries those facts is harder to misread and harder to dodge.

The loop runs the other way, too. Every release is a lead sheet for the next request.

Step 1: Identify the agency’s Microsoft 365 tenant and SharePoint hosts

Many federal agencies run on Microsoft 365. Every Microsoft 365 customer has a tenant, and the tenant’s original name becomes its SharePoint address. Microsoft spells this out: “When you first sign up for Microsoft 365, you create an onmicrosoft.com domain. Even if you later add a custom domain, all your SharePoint and OneDrive URLs use the original onmicrosoft.com domain.”

So don’t guess the address from the agency’s email domain. Confirm it with three public checks. None of them touches the agency’s servers.

  1. Check DNS. Look up the candidate name, such as [tenant].sharepoint.com, through a public DNS-over-HTTPS service. A name that resolves exists. NXDOMAIN means no such name. Run a made-up control name too. If it fails and yours resolves, your result means something.
  2. Read Microsoft’s public sign-in metadata. Microsoft publishes an OpenID Connect discovery document for every tenant so that apps can sign users in. Ask for it by the agency’s email domain, and it returns the tenant’s ID. Ask again with [tenant].onmicrosoft.com. If the IDs match, the SharePoint name belongs to the agency. A region field reading “GCC” marks Microsoft’s government community cloud.
  3. Search passive DNS and certificate logs. Services such as SecurityTrails, owned by Recorded Future since January 2022, keep histories of DNS names. Certificate-transparency logs, searchable at crt.sh, list the certificates issued for an agency’s own domains. Both are better at finding an agency’s websites than its SharePoint, which sits behind Microsoft’s certificates.

For the command line

Three public lookups. None of them touches the agency’s servers.

# 1. Does the SharePoint name exist? (DNS-over-HTTPS; NXDOMAIN means no)
curl -s "https://dns.google/resolve?name=unitedstatessecretservice.sharepoint.com&type=A"

# 2. Which Microsoft tenant signs in this email domain, and in which cloud?
curl -s "https://login.microsoftonline.com/secretservice.gov/v2.0/.well-known/openid-configuration" \
  | jq -r .issuer,.tenant_region_sub_scope

# 3. Does the tenant's original name map to the same tenant?
curl -s "https://login.microsoftonline.com/unitedstatessecretservice.onmicrosoft.com/v2.0/.well-known/openid-configuration" \
  | jq -r .issuer

If the issuer lines match, the domains share one tenant. A GCC sub-scope marks Microsoft’s government community cloud.

Know what each result proves. A name that resolves shows that a Microsoft customer by that name exists. It does not show who the customer is. Anyone can register a tenant with a government-sounding name. The tenant-ID match is what ties a SharePoint name to an agency’s own email domains. A name that fails to resolve is a dead end for now, but it may be a typo, a renamed tenant or a retired host. Check again before you write it into a request.

Example

The Secret Service tenant

The Secret Service’s email domain is secretservice.gov. Its SharePoint is not usss.sharepoint.com. That name does not exist. It is unitedstatessecretservice.sharepoint.com, with personal OneDrive storage at unitedstatessecretservice-my.sharepoint.com.

Both Secret Service names resolve. usss.sharepoint.com and a made-up control name return NXDOMAIN. For secretservice.gov, usss.dhs.gov and unitedstatessecretservice.onmicrosoft.com, the tenant ID in Microsoft’s sign-in metadata is the same, and the region field reads “GCC.”

Step 2: Identify the custodian by name or title

You don’t need an email address. Agencies search by custodian, the person whose records are at issue, and a name with a title is enough. If you don’t know the name, a title can work; Cause of Action Institute named two of its officials by position. A name and title together leave the least room for error.

Start with the agency’s own words. Leadership pages, testimony, org charts and press releases give titles and dates. Those dates matter, because your request should cover a period when the person held the job.

FOIA logs are a second source. Many agencies post them, and they show which custodians other requesters have named and what came back.

If a release shows a username, note it. Don’t build on it. An address pattern guessed from one partial username is a guess, and your request doesn’t need it.

Example

Naming Ronald L. Rowe Jr.

The 2023 requests named “Ronald L. Rowe, Jr., Deputy Director,” and the agency found his records. The Secret Service announced his appointment in an April 18, 2023, release that said, “His tenure will begin in the coming months.” His congressional witness biography from December 2024 says he was “Appointed in April 2023.”

Titles change, and the request should follow them. In the fall of 2023, Rowe was the deputy director, and the requests said so. By April 2025, he was no longer acting director; Sean M. Curran took over the agency on Jan. 22, 2025. That request named Rowe as the custodian for documents and a year of card statements covering 2024, when he served as deputy director and then as acting director. The release page shows a username beginning “ron.rowe.” Neither request needed it.

Step 3: Choose the record types

Once you know the tenant and the person, these records come into reach. Ask for each one by the system that holds it.

Browser history and web-proxy logs

Ask for both the history and the logs. Request the browser history itself, and request any web-proxy, secure-gateway, DNS or endpoint logs that record what the custodian’s accounts and devices visited. Cause of Action turned partly on employees’ freedom to delete their own histories. A log kept centrally by the agency’s network or security tools stands on different footing.

Example

What the Secret Service returned

Each of the five 2023 requests asked first for “A copy of the individual’s browser history (whether exported from Chrome, Safari, Windows Explorer, Mozilla, etc), to include all file attachments regardless of type and the entire email chain for completeness.”

The Rowe page is not a file exported from a browser. It has host names but no page addresses, a username column and background Microsoft traffic. It looks more like a log kept by the agency’s network or security tools than a history stored on one laptop. The agency sent the records on paper. By the author’s count, the stack ran about 500 pages.

Editor’s note: The 10/4/2023 rows on the Rowe page list host names for ad auctions and ad verification, among them fastlane.rubiconproject.com, ssum-sec.casalemedia.com, eb2.3lift.com, match.adsrvr.org, acdn.adnxs.com, x.bidswitch.net and cdn.doubleverify.com. These are the kind of real-time-bidding systems explained in OctoProbe’s report on DHS and phone location data. The log shows only that a browser on the account contacted them. It does not show what was read, what was shown or what data left the session.

SharePoint documents created and edited

Ask for documents by who made or changed them. Microsoft’s eDiscovery tools, which agencies use to search their own systems, track a document’s “Created by” value and “Last modified by,” described as “The user who last modified the document.” The audit log records “Modified file” and “Uploaded file” events, among others, according to Microsoft’s activity list.

Name both hosts: the main SharePoint and the custodian’s OneDrive at [tenant]-my.sharepoint.com. Ask for the documents themselves, sorted by last-modified date, over a fixed period. Step 5 explains why.

For documents a custodian commented on, ask for the documents. OctoProbe found no comment event for SharePoint files in Microsoft’s audit activity list, so a request for a comment log may come back empty even when the documents exist.

Teams messages, channel files and chat files

Teams chats are easy to miss in a records search. Microsoft’s eDiscovery guide says “Teams data is stored in Azure Cosmos DB. Exchange Online stores Teams compliance records” that eDiscovery searches. In practice, chat messages are copied into mailboxes, and shared files land in OneDrive or SharePoint. Name each place:

  • Chats (1:1 and group): the custodian’s Exchange Online mailbox.
  • Channel posts: the team’s mailbox, by team name.
  • Channel files: the team’s SharePoint site.
  • Files shared in chats: the sharer’s OneDrive.

Example

Other requesters already ask

At least a dozen entries in the Secret Service’s FY 2024 log seek “any chats in the relevant virtual platforms (e.g., Microsoft Teams, Zoom, Webex, etc.).” You can go further by naming the custodian’s mailbox for chats, the team’s mailbox and SharePoint site for channels and the custodian’s OneDrive for files shared in chats.

Card statements and sitemaps

Two more records round out a request. An official’s agency card statements show spending. A sitemap of the agency’s SharePoint shows how the agency organizes its work, and its site names tell you which offices and systems to name next.

Example

The 2025 request

The Secret Service received a four-part request on April 21, 2025. It asked for the last 100 documents Rowe edited and the last 100 he created on the agency’s SharePoint, a year of his agency card statements and a current sitemap of the agency’s SharePoint. Each item named the agency’s real SharePoint addresses. The release ran 117 pages. It held a sitemap of roughly 660 internal sites and 105 pages of blacked-out card statements. The PDF contains no records for the two document items.

Step 4: Write the request

Keep each item to one sentence. Give every item a date range, a custodian with a title and a system. Then add the parts that decide cost and speed.

  • Scope and date range. One record type per numbered item. A fixed start and end date on each.
  • Custodian. Full name and title.
  • Systems. SharePoint, OneDrive, Exchange Online and Teams, by host name where you have it.
  • Format. FOIA requires agencies to provide records “in any form or format requested by the person if the record is readily reproducible” in that form. Ask for electronic copies or native files.
  • Fee category. If you’re a reporter, say so. Fees for “a representative of the news media” are limited to “reasonable standard charges for document duplication” when records are not sought for commercial use. The law defines that requester as one who “gathers information of potential interest to a segment of the public, uses its editorial skills to turn the raw materials into a distinct work, and distributes that work to an audience.”
  • Fee waiver. Ask for one if the records are newsworthy. The standard is disclosure that “is likely to contribute significantly to public understanding of the operations or activities of the government and is not primarily in the commercial interest of the requester.” Explain how you will publish.
  • Expedited processing, where it applies. A requester “primarily engaged in disseminating information” can ask for it by showing “urgency to inform the public concerning actual or alleged Federal Government activity.” The request must be “certified by such person to be true and correct to the best of such person’s knowledge and belief.” Use it only when the urgency is real.
  • A search description. Ask the agency to describe where it looked. A “no records” answer means more when you know.

Example

The 2025 wording

The Secret Service’s letter quotes the request’s first item this way: “The last 100 documents edited by the custodian on the Secret Service Sharepoint (https://unitedstatessecretservice.sharepoint.com OR https://unitedstatessecretservice-my.sharepoint.com).” The second item was the same, with “created” in place of “edited.” The fourth asked for “A current sitemap of the Secret Service Sharepoint (https://unitedstatessecretservice.sharepoint.com).” The request did this in four numbered items, and the response letter restates them.

Sample request language

Open with one line, number one item per record and close with format and fees. Replace every bracketed field. For documents and sitemaps, adapt the 2025 wording above. The templates below are OctoProbe’s, built on the statute’s wording.

Under the Freedom of Information Act, 5 U.S.C. § 552, I request the following records:

Browser history and web logs.

The web browsing history of [CUSTODIAN], [TITLE], from [START] to [END], including any centrally retained web proxy, secure web gateway, DNS or endpoint logs that record the URLs or hostnames visited from [CUSTODIAN]’s agency accounts or devices.

Documents commented on.

Documents on the [AGENCY] SharePoint or OneDrive (https://[TENANT].sharepoint.com OR https://[TENANT]-my.sharepoint.com) on which [CUSTODIAN] added or replied to a comment from [START] to [END].

Teams messages.

All Microsoft Teams messages sent or received by [CUSTODIAN], [TITLE], from [START] to [END], including 1:1 chats, group chats and channel posts, containing any of these terms: [KEYWORDS]. This includes the copies of those messages kept in [CUSTODIAN]’s Exchange Online mailbox, and files [CUSTODIAN] shared in those chats as stored in [CUSTODIAN]’s OneDrive (https://[TENANT]-my.sharepoint.com).

Card statements.

[CUSTODIAN]’s agency credit card statements from [START] to [END], including statements available through the agency’s card-program portal.

Format and search.

Please provide the records in electronic form, as native files where available, under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3)(B). Please describe the systems and locations searched.

News-media fee category.

I am a representative of the news media, as defined in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(A)(ii), and I seek these records for news gathering, not for commercial use. Please limit any fees to duplication charges.

Fee waiver.

I request a waiver of all fees under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(A)(iii). Disclosure is in the public interest because it is likely to contribute significantly to public understanding of the operations or activities of the government, and it is not primarily in my commercial interest. [Explain how and where you will publish.]

Expedited processing.

I request expedited processing under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(6)(E). I am primarily engaged in disseminating information, and there is an urgency to inform the public concerning actual or alleged Federal Government activity: [explain the urgency]. I certify that this statement is true and correct to the best of my knowledge and belief.

A few rules of thumb:

  • Use keywords for Teams and email. A year of chats with no search terms invites a delay or a fee estimate.
  • Split big asks into separate numbered items, so one problem item doesn’t stall the rest.
  • Keep the request about records, not people’s private lives. Expect exemptions (b)(6) and (b)(7)(C) on names and personal details.

Step 5: Anticipate the agency’s answers

Three answers come back again and again. Plan for each before you file.

“Exempt”

In the Secret Service releases, three codes cover nearly everything withheld. Exemption (b)(6) protects files whose release would be “a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy.” Exemption (b)(7)(C) protects law-enforcement records whose release “could reasonably be expected to constitute an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy.” Exemption (b)(7)(E) protects material that “would disclose techniques and procedures for law enforcement investigations or prosecutions.”

How to word around them: ask for records about official work, not private details. Expect names of staff and account numbers to come out blacked out. Ask for what a withheld column would show in the aggregate, such as site names without addresses, when the addresses are the sensitive part.

Example

How the Secret Service used them

The first two codes explain the boxes over names and account digits. The third is the agency’s basis for withholding the sitemap’s URLs and much of the card statements. On the sitemap, the agency withheld the entire URL column under (b)(7)(E) on every page. It released the names.

“No duty to create a record”

The legal risk is the word “list.” FOIA requires agencies to “make reasonable efforts to search for the records in electronic form or format,” and to provide records “in any form or format requested … if the record is readily reproducible.” Courts have said a database query is a search. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit held in 2021 that “using a query to search for and extract a particular arrangement or subset of data already maintained in an agency’s database does not amount to the creation of a new record.” The court added, “It makes no difference if the query produces a set of documents, a list, a spreadsheet, or some other form of results that the agency has not previously viewed.”

A federal judge in Washington drew a line in 2012. The court held that “sorting a pre-existing database of information to make information intelligible does not involve the creation of a new record.” But a request for a “listing or index of a database’s contents that does not seek the contents of the database, but instead essentially seeks information about those contents, is a request that requires the creation of a new record, insofar as the agency has not previously created and retained such a listing or index.” In a later ruling in the same litigation, the D.C. Circuit sided with the CIA on a request for one such listing.

That is a real risk for both “last 100” requests and sitemap requests. How to word around it: ask for the documents themselves, sorted by date, over a fixed period, not a list of them. For a sitemap, ask for an existing site inventory or admin report if one exists.

Where the objection bites

The PDF contains no records for items 1 and 2, the last 100 documents the custodian edited and created. The Secret Service, for its part, did produce a sitemap.

“Not an agency record”

Under FOIA, the public can get “agency records.” The law defines a record broadly, as information “maintained by an agency in any format, including an electronic format.” The harder question is whether something on a government computer belongs to the agency at all.

In 2021, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit said browsing histories did not, at least in one case. A watchdog group, Cause of Action Institute, had asked the Office of Management and Budget and the Agriculture Department for senior officials’ web histories. The court ruled for the agencies.

“In light of the foregoing, we hold that the internet browsing histories requested by Cause of Action are not agency records subject to disclosure under FOIA.”

The court’s reasons were practical. “OMB and USDA did not use the officials’ browsing histories for any purpose, much less a purpose connected to agency decisionmaking,” the court wrote. And “employees of OMB and USDA could freely delete their browsing histories, a practice incompatible with integration of these documents into the agencies’ record systems.”

Cause of Action turned on control and use: whether the agency kept the data, relied on it and let employees erase it. A centrally kept web log is a different animal. The National Archives’ general records schedule for IT systems treats “log-in files,” “audit trail files and extracts” and “system usage files” as federal records with a set disposition: “Destroy when business use ceases.” The schedule does not mention browser history by name, and it carves out monitoring for agency missions such as law enforcement. But it shows that logs of this kind are records an agency is expected to manage.

How to word around it: ask for the browser history and for any centrally retained proxy, gateway, DNS or endpoint logs in the same item. If the agency calls the history personal, the logs are a separate question.

Example

The Secret Service’s practice points the other way

On five requests received the same day, the Secret Service searched for an official’s browser history, processed what it found and released it in part, withholding some information under privacy and law-enforcement exemptions. The Rowe page looks more like a centrally kept log than a history stored on one laptop.

Step 6: Track and follow up

Write down the date the agency received your request and the log number it assigns. The clock starts there. Agencies must “determine within 20 days (excepting Saturdays, Sundays, and legal public holidays)” whether to comply. The law lets agencies extend that deadline in “unusual circumstances,” and they must tell you in writing when they do.

Check the agency’s FOIA log. Many agencies post them. A log entry confirms what the agency recorded as your request, when it arrived and how it closed. It also shows you how other requesters worded similar asks.

Example

The Secret Service’s FY 2024 log

The Secret Service’s FOIA log for fiscal 2024, posted on the agency’s website, lists five requests received Nov. 3, 2023, from the author. Each sought records “from the last month of October 2023” for one official: Director Kimberly A. Cheatle; “Ronald L. Rowe, Jr., Deputy Director”; Chief Operating Officer Cynthia Sjoberg Radway; David M. Smith, assistant director for investigations; and Gloria R. Armstrong, assistant director for professional responsibility. The log records the result for all five in three words: “Granted/Denied in Part.” The photographed page comes from the return for Rowe, request 20240110. Its dates, Oct. 4 and Oct. 10, fall inside the request’s October window.

Watch the disposal clock. The National Archives’ schedule lets agencies destroy many system logs “when business use ceases,” so a request for last month’s web log has better odds than one for last year’s. File log requests fast.

If you hit a dispute you can’t resolve with the FOIA office, the Office of Government Information Services at the National Archives is one option. The law says OGIS “shall offer mediation services to resolve disputes between persons making requests under this section and administrative agencies as a nonexclusive alternative to litigation.”

Step 7: Use what comes back

Each release is raw material. Read it for names, systems and redaction patterns, and turn them into the next set of requests.

  • Sites become systems. A site that holds audit logs or a card-program portal points to records you can name.
  • Hosts become clouds. Microsoft host names in a log confirm which cloud the agency runs in.
  • Hubs become offices. Each hub in a sitemap is an office with its own records, staff and leaders. Those leaders are your next custodians.
  • Withholdings become questions. An entire column withheld under (b)(7)(E) tells you what the agency considers sensitive. That is worth knowing when you write the next request.

The release is 117 image-only pages. The PDF itself holds no cover letter; the agency’s response letter, sent separately, opens by restating the four items.

Example

The next round

“SP 2016 Audit Logs” suggests the agency keeps SharePoint audit records in a site of their own. Ask for the audit entries for a named custodian and date range. “Travel Card Portal” points to the card program’s guidance and records. The Rowe page lists gcc.thor.aesir.office.com and other Microsoft government-cloud hosts. That confirms what the sign-in metadata showed: the agency runs in Microsoft’s government community cloud.

Two lessons follow. First, a sitemap request can work even with the addresses withheld, because the site names tell you which offices and record systems to name next. Second, the document items are where the listing-or-index objection bites. A future request should ask for the documents, sorted by last-modified date, over a fixed period.

Then map again. The method doesn’t end with one release.

Before you file

  • You confirmed the tenant: DNS resolves, and the tenant ID matches the agency’s email domain.
  • You named both hosts: [tenant].sharepoint.com and [tenant]-my.sharepoint.com.
  • Every item names a custodian by full name and title, with the dates the person held the job.
  • Every item has a fixed start and end date.
  • Each record type is its own numbered item, tied to the system that holds it.
  • You asked for documents sorted by date, not a list or index of them.
  • Teams and email items carry keywords.
  • Browser-history items also ask for centrally retained proxy, gateway, DNS or endpoint logs.
  • You asked for electronic copies or native files and a description of the search.
  • You stated your fee category and, if it fits, asked for a fee waiver or expedited processing.
  • You are filing log requests soon after the period you care about.
  • You will record the receipt date and log number, then check the agency’s FOIA log.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an official’s browser history an agency record under FOIA?

It depends on the agency. In Cause of Action Institute v. OMB (2021), the D.C. Circuit held that browsing histories at the Office of Management and Budget and the Agriculture Department were not agency records, because the agencies did not use them and employees could delete them. The Secret Service, by contrast, processed five requests for senior officials’ browser histories and closed each as granted or denied in part.

Do I need an official’s email address to request their records?

No. Agencies search by custodian. A full name and title, such as “Ronald L. Rowe Jr., Deputy Director,” is enough. Do not guess an email address; if a release shows a username, quote only what the agency released.

Can an agency refuse a “last 100 documents” request as creating a new record?

It may try. Courts have held that running a search or query on existing data is not creating a record, but a federal judge in Washington ruled in 2012 that a bare listing or index of a database’s contents can be. Ask for the documents themselves, sorted by last-modified date, rather than a list.

Where does Microsoft store Teams chats?

Microsoft’s eDiscovery documentation says messages in 1:1 and group chats are stored in the Exchange Online mailbox of every chat participant, channel posts in the mailbox associated with the team, files shared in chats in the sharer’s OneDrive and channel files in the team’s SharePoint site.

What do FOIA exemptions (b)(6), (b)(7)(C) and (b)(7)(E) mean?

Exemption (b)(6) covers files whose release would be a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy. (b)(7)(C) covers law-enforcement records whose release could reasonably be expected to invade personal privacy. (b)(7)(E) covers law-enforcement techniques and procedures. The Secret Service used all three in the releases described here.

How long do agencies keep web and system logs?

Many logs are short-lived. The National Archives’ general schedule for system access records, including log-in files and system usage files, allows agencies to destroy them when business use ceases. File requests for logs soon after the period you care about.
